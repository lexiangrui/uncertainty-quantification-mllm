\subsection{The Low-Uncertainty Hallucination Blind Spot}
\label{subsec:luh_blind_spot}

The preceding results reveal a central contradiction: all three baseline scores detect hallucination systematically less effectively than error. I first analyze the methodological cause of this gap, then quantify the blind spot, and finally illustrate its forms through representative cases.

\subsubsection{Why UQ Fails to Predict Hallucination}

The baselines share a mechanism assumption: an uncertain answer should have low probability, dispersed samples, or unstable internal representations, allowing its unsupported nature to be inferred from the score. This assumption requires an observable difference between the model's predictive distributions for grounded and ungrounded responses. Low-uncertainty hallucination violates that requirement.

\textbf{Model overconfidence disables sampling-based signals.} When a model forms a stable, high-confidence prediction of hallucinated content, its sampling distribution becomes highly concentrated: nearly all probability mass falls on one answer, repeated samples are semantically identical, and their internal representations converge. All three scores then indicate extreme certainty. PPL approaches 1, Semantic Entropy approaches 0, and both the semantic-volume and incoherence terms of UMPIRE become small. The scores mistake stability for reliability, even though the model is confidently reproducing the same unsupported content. UQ measures how certain the model is, not whether it ought to be certain. Greater consistency can therefore produce even lower uncertainty for an overconfident hallucination and cause systematic false negatives.

This mechanism explains the target gap in \Cref{tab:target_gap}. The weaker hallucination performance is not merely a metric-selection issue; answer-space signals fail in principle on overconfident unsupported content. Identifying these cases requires testing whether stable content is supported by evidence rather than merely whether the answer itself is stable.

\subsubsection{Quantifying the Missed-Hallucination Share}

For each model, pooling its evaluated samples across the three datasets, I define the low-uncertainty hallucination share using the $\alpha$-quantile $Q_\alpha(H_0)$ of the non-hallucinated score distribution:
\[
\operatorname{luh\_share}(\alpha)
=\Pr\!\left(s_{H_1}\leq Q_\alpha(H_0)\right).
\]
It is the fraction of hallucinated samples whose scores do not exceed the corresponding non-hallucinated quantile. \Cref{tab:luh_share} reports results for $\alpha\in\{0.25,0.50\}$.

\subsection{Low-Uncertainty Subset Extraction and Validation}
\label{subsec:luh_subset}

The preceding analysis establishes the LUH blind spot both mechanistically and quantitatively. To support the design and evaluation of the improved method in Experiment~2, I construct a difficult subset for each model. Hallucinated positives and non-hallucinated negatives are deliberately matched to overlap strongly in all three baseline scores, preventing the baselines from distinguishing them by uncertainty alone.

\subsubsection{Extraction Procedure}

For each model, I pool samples from all three datasets, excluding HallusionBench non-image samples because the subsequent method requires visual tokens, and perform the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \textbf{Three-dimensional percentiles.} For every valid sample, compute average-rank percentiles for each method, producing $(p^{\mathrm{PPL}},p^{\mathrm{SE}},p^{\mathrm{UMPIRE}})$; ties receive their average rank.
\item \textbf{Positive selection.} Among $H=1$ samples, select the 200 lowest values of $\bar p=\frac{1}{3}\sum_m p^m$. These are the hallucinations that all three methods consider most certain and constitute the LUH positive class.
\item \textbf{Negative matching.} For each positive, greedily select the closest unused $H=0$ sample by Euclidean distance in the three-dimensional percentile space, producing 200 matched negatives.
\item \textbf{Subset construction.} Combine the positives and negatives into a 400-sample low-uncertainty subset.
\end{enumerate}

Matching in three dimensions, rather than by the average alone, produces substantial class overlap for every baseline and drives the fixed-direction AUROC toward random performance or mild reverse ordering. The extracted records are stored in \path{results/analysis/luh/per_model_subsets.json} and in each model's \path{model_subset_ids.txt} file.

Across the nine model-method combinations, AUROC ranges from 0.417 to 0.502. All six Qwen and InternVL values lie between 0.482 and 0.502 and are effectively random. LLaVA PPL and UMPIRE are below 0.5 (0.423 and 0.417), indicating mild reverse ordering under the fixed score direction rather than useful positive hallucination detection. Score distributions agree with this interpretation: on the LLaVA subset, the positive/negative means are 1.0013/1.0019 for PPL, 0.238/0.258 for SE, and $-36.03/-32.93$ for UMPIRE. Without flipping a score direction post hoc on this constructed subset, the baselines cannot distinguish hallucinated from non-hallucinated samples using UQ scores alone. These fixed difficult subsets therefore support Experiment~2, although results on them should be interpreted together with full-data performance because the subsets are explicitly constructed against the three baselines.
